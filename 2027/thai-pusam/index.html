Thai Pusam 2027 (Thaipusam)

Thai Pusam falls on Friday, 22 January 2027 — the full-moon day of the Tamil month Thai — when devotees carry kavadi to Murugan temples in Tamil Nadu, Malaysia and Singapore.

Date: Friday, 22 January 2027
Tamil month: Thai · Pusam nakshatra
Deity: Lord Murugan (Skanda)
Main centres: Palani · Vadapalani · Batu Caves

Thai Pusam 2027 Date and Time (IST, Chennai)

Thai Pusam 2027 is observed on Friday, 22 January 2027. The festival is fixed by the Pusam nakshatra coinciding with the full moon in the Tamil month of Thai, rather than by a fixed Gregorian date. All dates on this page follow Indian Standard Time (IST), calculated for Chennai as the reference location — the same convention used throughout this site. Because tithi and nakshatra endings are computed from sunrise at a particular place, the observed day can shift by a few hours, and occasionally by a full day, in countries far from the Chennai meridian.

For devotees in Tamil Nadu this presents no difficulty: the 22nd is the day to plan for. Families in Malaysia and Singapore should check the local temple announcement, as the date is usually the same but the kavadi procession times are set by the local temple committee. Always confirm the exact Pusam timing with your own temple or a local panchangam before travelling.

The Legend: Parvati's Spear and Murugan

The story at the heart of Thaipusam tells of the birth of Murugan, the son of Shiva and Parvati. The gods were troubled by the demon Tarakasura, who could only be defeated by Shiva's son. Parvati performed severe penance to bring about that son, and when Murugan was born he was given a divine spear — the vel — forged from Parvati's own energy. The spear is not merely a weapon in Tamil tradition: it represents the power of devotion, the piercing of ignorance and the defeat of arrogance. Murugan is worshipped as the ever-youthful god who protects his devotees and answers their prayers.

Thai Pusam marks the moment when the goddess's gift was received and the young god set out to fulfil his purpose. The vel is carried in processions, decorated with flowers and peacock feathers, and invoked in the chants that accompany the kavadi. In temples the festival icon is taken out in a chariot, and the spear is honoured with lamp offerings and abhishekam. Understanding the legend helps explain why the day is observed with such intensity: the devotee who carries a kavadi is re-enacting a journey of surrender, not simply performing a spectacle.

Kavadi, Fasting and the Meaning of the Vow

A kavadi is a decorated burden carried on the shoulders — a wooden or steel arch hung with flowers, peacock feathers and pots of milk, fastened to the body with ropes or, in the strictest forms, with hooks. Devotees take a vow (vratam) to carry it as an act of gratitude or supplication, often after a period of fasting and prayer lasting days or weeks. The fast is usually partial: many devotees eat only once a day, avoid non-vegetarian food, alcohol and onions, and sleep apart from their families. On the morning of Thai Pusam they bathe, dress in yellow or red, and gather at the temple in a state of ritual purity.

The physical weight of the kavadi is symbolic. It stands for the devotee's own ego and burden, offered back to Murugan. Some carry a simpler pal kavadi, a pot of milk balanced on the head; others undertake more elaborate forms that demand strict discipline and should never be attempted without the guidance of experienced devotees and temple authorities. The fast is broken only after the evening puja and the return of the kavadi to the temple. The vow is considered complete when the burden has been carried and the prasad shared with family and friends.

Three Great Celebrations: Palani, Vadapalani and Batu Caves

Palani, in the Dindigul district of Tamil Nadu, is the most important Thai Pusam centre in India. The hill temple of Murugan draws enormous crowds, and the approach roads are lined with devotees walking barefoot up the steps. The kavadi processions begin in the early morning and continue through the day, with the temple staying open late into the night. Nearby towns fill with pilgrims, and the atmosphere is both solemn and electric.

Vadapalani in Chennai is the city's principal Murugan temple and the focus of Thai Pusam within the capital. Long processions move through the surrounding streets, accompanied by nadaswaram, drums and chanting. Roads around the temple are closed to traffic for much of the day, and the queues to enter the sanctum can stretch for hours. Devotees who cannot travel to Palani often fulfil their vow here.

Batu Caves near Kuala Lumpur is the largest Thaipusam gathering in the world. Tens of thousands of devotees, many of them of Tamil descent, climb the 272 coloured steps to the cave temple carrying kavadi. The procession begins at the Sri Subramaniar temple at the foot of the hill and winds through the city before the ascent. For the Malaysian and Singaporean Tamil community, Thaipusam is a defining annual event — a public expression of faith that fills the streets from dawn to late evening.

Thai Pusam, Thaipusam, Thai Poosam: Three Spellings

The same festival appears in English under three spellings, and all three are correct. Thai Pusam is the common transliteration in Tamil Nadu and the form used in most Tamil almanacs and English-language temple notices. Thai Poosam is the variant found in older Tamil Nadu guidebooks and some government holiday lists — the doubled "oo" simply marks the long vowel in the Tamil word. Thaipusam is the spelling preferred by Malaysian and Singaporean media and official tourism sources, where the two Tamil words are joined into one. Searching for any one spelling can miss useful information, so this guide uses all three deliberately: Thai Pusam for the date and ritual details, Thai Poosam where Tamil Nadu sources use it, and Thaipusam when describing the Batu Caves celebration.

Practical Notes: Fasting, Vows and Road Closures

Plan the day around the temple rather than the clock. Devotees who have taken a vow arrive early, usually before sunrise, and should eat a light meal beforehand unless their vow requires a full fast. Carry water, wear comfortable clothing that can be tied at the waist, and leave valuables at home — the crowds are dense and the kavadi needs both hands. Remove footwear before entering temple premises, and follow the queue system; temple volunteers and police manage the flow, and pushing ahead is both unsafe and disrespectful.

Road closures are routine. In Chennai, streets around Vadapalani are closed from the early morning and reopen only after the evening procession; in Palani, the hill roads are restricted and pilgrims walk the final stretch. At Batu Caves, the staircase and surrounding roads are controlled by police, and visitors without a kavadi are routed separately. Check the temple's social media or local news on the morning itself. If you are accompanying a devotee, agree on a meeting point in advance — mobile reception fails in the crush. The fast ends after the return of the kavadi and the distribution of prasad; families usually share a meal together in the evening.

Visiting With Family

Not everyone needs to carry a kavadi. Many families attend simply to watch the processions, offer flowers and receive prasad. Children should be kept close and away from the path of the processions, which move faster than expected. Elderly visitors may find the Palani steps difficult; the temple provides seating and water points along the route. Photography is welcome in public processions but restricted inside the sanctum.
