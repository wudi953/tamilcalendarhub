Vinayagar Chathurthi 2027 (Ganesh Chaturthi)

Vinayagar Chathurthi falls on Saturday, 4 September 2027 — the fourth day of Aavani — when clay idols of Ganesha are installed in homes and street pandals across Tamil Nadu.

Date: Saturday, 4 September 2027
Tamil month: Aavani · Chathurthi tithi
Deity: Lord Ganesha (Vinayagar)
Also called: Ganesh Chaturthi · Vinayaka Chaturthi

Vinayagar Chathurthi 2027 Date and Time (IST, Chennai)

Vinayagar Chathurthi 2027 is observed on Saturday, 4 September 2027, the fourth day (Chathurthi tithi) of the Tamil month Aavani, the waning fortnight. All dates on this page follow Indian Standard Time (IST), calculated for Chennai as the reference location — the convention used throughout this site. The festival is tied to a lunar tithi rather than a fixed Gregorian date, so temple announcements in other countries can differ by a day when the tithi begins close to sunrise.

The same festival is known by several names, and all of them point to the same observance. Vinayagar Chathurthi is the Tamil form used in Tamil Nadu's almanacs and government holiday lists. Ganesh Chaturthi is the widely used North Indian and international name, and Vinayaka Chaturthi is the common Sanskrit-derived variant used in temples and by the diaspora. If you are searching for the 2027 date, checking all three spellings avoids missing local guidance.

The Birth of Ganesha and What the Day Means

Ganesha is the son of Shiva and Parvati, the remover of obstacles and the god of beginnings. The best-known story tells how Parvati created him from turmeric paste to guard her while she bathed, and how Shiva, returning unseen, struck off the boy's head. When Parvati's grief revealed who the child was, Shiva restored him with the head of an elephant and granted him a place of honour among the gods. The story is retold on Chathurthi with a mixture of affection and awe: Ganesha is at once the mischievous child who loves sweets and the powerful deity who must be invoked before any new venture.

The festival therefore marks more than a birthday. It is a recognition that obstacles are part of every undertaking and that humility and wisdom are needed to pass them. Households begin new account books, start studies or simply worship together on this day. In Tamil tradition Ganesha is also called Pillaiyar, the "great child", and the Chathurthi observance is one of the most widely kept home festivals of the year, observed equally in villages, apartments and temples abroad.

The Clay Idol and the Shift Towards Eco-Friendly Worship

At the centre of the celebration is the clay idol. Families either buy a small pisinu pillaiyar made from clay or shape one at home, then decorate it with flowers, leaves, turmeric, rice flour and paint. The idol is installed on a clean platform, offered lamp and incense, and worshipped through the day. In recent years temples and municipalities have actively promoted clay over plaster of Paris: clay dissolves naturally when immersed in a tank or the sea, while plaster idols leave chemical paint and plastic accessories in the water.

This change has been driven by both environmental concern and practical regulation. Many local authorities now provide dedicated immersion tanks, ban chemical colours and encourage devotees to use natural dyes and offerings that decompose. The older practice of keeping an idol for a few days before immersion continues, but the material has changed. Families who cannot immerse at home can take the idol to a community point organised by the temple or the municipality. The shift is widely described as a return to older habits rather than a new invention — clay was the original material before plaster became common.

Kozhu Kattai, Ellu Urundai and the Food of the Festival

The food of Vinayagar Chathurthi is as distinctive as the idol. Kozhu kattai (also spelt kozhukattai) are dumplings of rice flour stuffed with a sweet mixture of jaggery and coconut, or a savoury filling of steamed lentils; they are said to resemble the elephant-shaped form of Ganesha and are his principal offering. Ellu urundai — sesame balls bound with jaggery — are made in most Tamil households and distributed to neighbours and visitors. Both are prepared at home in the morning, offered at the altar after the puja, and then shared as prasad.

Banana leaves, payasam, sundal, banana and tender coconut complete the meal. Many families observe a fast until the evening puja, breaking it only after the offerings have been made. The preparation itself is part of the observance: grinding the lentils, rolling the rice flour into shells and shaping the sesame balls are tasks done together by several generations, and the failure of a dumpling to close is treated with good-humoured superstition rather than alarm.

Chathurthi Tithi: Sunset, Moonrise and the Two Rules

The timing rules of Chathurthi are among the most discussed parts of the festival. The observance is governed by the fourth lunar day, and two traditional conditions determine when the puja and the seeing of the moon should take place. The first is that the Chathurthi tithi should be present at sunset; the second is that the moon should be seen after it has risen and is clearly visible, never before. Where the tithi straddles sunset, priests consult a panchangam to decide which day carries the observance.

The rule about the moon is widely known: looking at the moon on Chathurthi before the proper time is traditionally believed to invite false accusations or misunderstanding, and devotees who do so are said to offer water to the moon as a remedy in the evening. These are matters of custom rather than astronomy, and practice varies between families and temples. For 2027 the date given here follows the Chennai panchangam calculation; if you live outside Tamil Nadu, ask your temple which sunset and moonrise times it is using before observing the fast or the moon-viewing.

Street Pandals, Processions and the Immersion

In towns and cities, Vinayagar Chathurthi is a public festival. Neighbourhoods build decorated pandals — temporary arches and canopies of coloured cloth, lights and flowers — and install a larger idol for everyone to worship. Evenings bring music, cultural programmes and food stalls, and the streets fill with families walking from one pandal to the next. In Chennai, Coimbatore and Madurai, the larger displays draw visitors from across the city, and local committees compete quietly over the most imaginative decoration.

The festival closes with immersion. The idol is taken in a procession to a tank, river or the sea, accompanied by drums and chanting, and returned to the water in the late afternoon or evening. The act symbolises Ganesha's return to the cosmic elements after his visit, and the community disperses with prasad. Processions can close roads for an evening, so check local traffic notices and use public transport where possible. If you are carrying a home idol, go early to the designated immersion point and follow the queue; the crowds are heaviest just before sunset.

Planning for the Day

Temples are crowded from the early morning, and the pandal routes are busiest in the evening. Wear comfortable footwear for walking between displays, and remove it at each shrine. Families with children often visit in the afternoon, when the crowds are thinner, and return for the immersion later.
